Channels are where a go-to-market strategy becomes visible — and where most of its budget disappears. They are also where teams most often start, which is backwards: a channel is a delivery mechanism for decisions made elsewhere in the strategy, and no channel performs well when it is delivering an unclear message to an undefined audience. That is also the key to understanding lead generation's real relationship to GTM, which this article covers in its second half.

## The Most Common Go-to-Market Channels

The channel landscape sorts into five families, each with a distinct cost structure, speed, and failure mode.

**Owned inbound: search, content, and SEO.** The buyer arrives at the moment of problem-awareness, pre-qualified by their own query. Cost structure: labour-heavy, media-light, compounding — articles built this year produce pipeline for years. Speed: slow; three to nine months before meaningful signal. Failure mode: producing content for volume keywords instead of buying-intent keywords, which generates traffic that never converts. Inbound is the backbone channel for considered B2B purchases, and AI-assisted production has cut its cost dramatically — the economics are covered in [organic content strategy with AI](/insights/organic-content-strategy-ai-germany).

**Paid acquisition: search ads, paid social, display.** The only channel family that scales linearly with spend and produces signal within weeks, which makes it the standard validation instrument early in a GTM motion. Cost structure: pure variable cost, rented — pipeline stops when spend stops. Failure mode: scaling spend against unvalidated positioning, buying expensive proof that the message does not convert. In DACH B2B specifically, paid works best as a targeting scalpel (job titles, named accounts) rather than a volume engine.

**Outbound: SDR sequences, founder outreach, account-based campaigns.** Precision access to named accounts, independent of whether they were searching. Cost structure: dominated by salaries; expensive per touch, cheap per experiment. Speed: fast to start, slow to convert. Failure mode: volume-over-relevance — automated sequences to loosely matched lists, which burns the segment's goodwill along with the budget. Outbound quality is a direct function of ICP quality; it is the channel that punishes lazy segmentation fastest.

**Earned and social: PR, trade press, LinkedIn, communities, events.** Third-party credibility and peer visibility — disproportionately important in trust-driven markets. German-speaking B2B is the extreme case: a mention in the relevant trade publication outperforms most paid campaigns, and [LinkedIn in German B2B](/insights/linkedin-b2b-germany-2026) behaves as a practitioner-credibility channel rather than a broadcast channel. Cost structure: relationship- and time-intensive, hard to schedule. Failure mode: treating it as a launch-week stunt rather than a standing presence.

**Partner and ecosystem: resellers, integrations, marketplaces, referral networks.** Someone else's trust and distribution, in exchange for margin or mutual value. The highest-leverage channel family at maturity and the most commonly premature at launch — partners amplify a proven motion; they do not validate an unproven one. A partner cannot sell what you have not yet learned to sell yourself.

## Choosing and Sequencing Channels

Channel selection is three questions applied honestly. **Where does the ICP already pay attention?** Not where you wish they were — where the evidence (customer interviews, win/loss data, community observation) says they actually discover and evaluate products in your category. **What can you execute credibly now?** A channel requires a competence: outbound needs list quality and message craft; content needs genuine expertise and patience; paid needs conversion assets and measurement. A theoretically ideal channel executed poorly returns less than a mediocre channel executed well. **What can you afford to measure?** A channel whose results you cannot attribute even roughly is a faith-based expenditure; the practical limits of attribution, especially under GDPR, are covered in [B2B marketing attribution in Germany](/insights/marketing-attribution-b2b-germany).

Sequencing beats parallelism at launch: one primary channel funded to interpretability, one secondary, and a small experimental line — the same concentration logic argued in [GTM budgeting](/insights/go-to-market-budget). Add the next channel when the current one is either repeatable (documented, delegated, predictable CAC) or conclusively falsified. The portfolio matures toward a barbell: compounding owned channels as the base, paid and outbound as the throttle.

One more distinction that prevents bad channel post-mortems: separate the channel from the message and the offer. A "failed" channel test is usually one of three failures — wrong channel, wrong message in the right channel, or wrong offer behind a right message. Diagnose before deleting; some of the best-performing channels in a mature motion are ones that "failed" at launch behind a positioning that has since been fixed.

## Lead Generation and GTM Strategy: The Real Relationship

Lead generation is the operational layer where channel activity becomes named pipeline. The relationship to GTM strategy is strictly hierarchical, and getting the hierarchy backwards is one of the most common — and most expensive — confusions in B2B marketing.

**The GTM strategy defines what a lead is.** A lead is not a form fill; it is a person matching the ICP, in a buying situation the strategy described, showing intent the strategy predicted. Without that definition, lead generation optimises toward what is cheap to capture — ebook downloads, webinar registrants, contest entries — and the funnel fills with volume that sales correctly ignores. The written qualification bar, co-owned with sales, is the strategy's most direct control over lead-gen quality.

**Demand generation and lead capture are different jobs.** Most of your market — routinely 95% — is not buying this quarter. Demand generation builds category and vendor preference inside the ICP before intent exists; lead capture harvests the intent when it arrives. A GTM motion that only funds capture (bottom-of-funnel search ads, gated content, retargeting) is competing in an auction for the 5% who are in-market, against every competitor doing the same, with CAC rising accordingly. A motion that also funds demand creation gets chosen before the comparison shopping starts. The strategy sets this balance deliberately; lead-gen tactics cannot set it themselves.

**Lead-gen metrics are GTM diagnostics.** Cost per lead is a channel metric; lead-to-opportunity and opportunity-to-close rates are strategy metrics. When leads are cheap but do not convert, the usual suspects are upstream: the targeting is broader than the ICP, or the message that attracted the lead misdescribed the product. The full diagnostic chain runs through the funnel instrumentation described in [Go-to-Market Metrics](/insights/go-to-market-metrics).

**The nurture layer is where the two meet.** Between first touch and sales-readiness sits the machinery — scoring, routing, sequencing, follow-up SLAs — that converts channel output into sales input. This is unglamorous infrastructure with outsized returns: response time to an inbound request is one of the strongest conversion variables in B2B, and it is a process property, not a talent property. A modest automation stack handles it; [a small-team CRM and automation setup](/insights/crm-automation-small-marketing-team) covers the mechanics.

## A Note on Channel Economics Over Time

Channels are not static line items; each has a lifecycle, and the GTM strategy should plan for it rather than be surprised by it.

Paid channels saturate. The first tranche of spend reaches the most active, cheapest-to-convert slice of the segment; each additional tranche reaches colder inventory at higher cost. Rising CAC on a paid channel with stable conversion downstream is usually saturation, not decay — the correct response is often to hold spend at the efficient level and put the marginal budget elsewhere, not to "fix" the channel.

Owned channels compound, then plateau. Content and SEO build slowly, deliver their steepest returns in years two and three, and eventually flatten as the realistic keyword and topic territory gets covered. The plateau is not failure; it is the point where the channel converts from a growth engine into cheap standing infrastructure, and new growth must come from the next channel in the sequence.

Outbound decays with repetition. A segment can only be sequenced so many times before response rates fall and sender reputation follows. Outbound sustainability depends on list refresh, genuine message variation tied to trigger events, and — bluntly — a product worth interrupting someone about.

The portfolio implication: a healthy GTM motion at maturity is deliberately unbalanced, with compounding owned channels carrying an increasing share of pipeline over time while paid and outbound are held at their efficient levels as controllable throttles. Reviewing channel mix quarterly against this lifecycle logic — rather than defending last year's allocation — is one of the quiet disciplines that separates GTM operations that get cheaper with scale from ones that get more expensive.

The summary hierarchy: strategy defines the customer and the message; channels deliver the message where the customer already is; lead generation converts the resulting attention into named, qualified pipeline. When lead generation underperforms, audit upward through that chain before spending downward on more volume — in most cases the "lead-gen problem" was manufactured two layers above it. Fix the chain in order — customer, message, channel, capture — and each layer makes the one below it cheaper; fix it in reverse and every euro of additional volume amplifies the upstream mistake. That ordering is also the fastest diagnostic when a channel review turns contentious: before anyone argues about budgets or agencies, agree on which layer the evidence actually implicates, and most of the argument dissolves.
