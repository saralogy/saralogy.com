Most guides to building a go-to-market strategy describe an artefact — a deck with a segment slide, a positioning slide, a channel slide. This one describes a process, because the value of a GTM strategy is not the document. It is the sequence of decisions the document forces you to make explicitly rather than by default.

This guide covers the full arc: the questions to answer before you start, the step-by-step construction of the strategy, what belongs in the written document, and how long each stage realistically takes.

## The Questions to Answer Before You Start

A GTM strategy built on unexamined assumptions is a well-formatted guess. Before drafting anything, five questions need honest answers — and "we don't know yet" is an acceptable answer, provided it is written down as a risk rather than papered over.

**1. What evidence do we have that the problem is real and painful?** Not that the product is good — that the problem it solves ranks high enough on the buyer's priority list to displace something else in their budget. Evidence means customer conversations, support data from an adjacent product, search demand, or paid pilots. Enthusiasm from friendly contacts does not count.

**2. Who feels this problem most acutely, and who pays to fix it?** These are often different people. In B2B especially, the user, the economic buyer, and the decision committee can be three distinct audiences, and the GTM strategy must eventually address all of them.

**3. What does the buyer do today instead?** The status quo is your real competitor in most categories. If the current answer is a spreadsheet, an intern, or nothing, your positioning problem is different — and usually harder — than if you displace a named vendor.

**4. What can the business afford this motion to cost?** Available budget, acceptable payback period, and runway shape which motions are even eligible. A twelve-month enterprise sales cycle is not a strategy option for a company with ten months of runway.

**5. What would make us kill or pivot this plan?** Defining failure conditions before launch is the cheapest insurance available. Teams that skip this step end up relitigating the strategy every month based on whoever is loudest. The warning signs worth pre-registering are covered in [Why Go-to-Market Strategies Fail](/insights/why-go-to-market-strategies-fail).

## Step by Step: Building the Strategy

With those questions answered, the construction sequence matters. Each step consumes the outputs of the previous one, which is why teams that start with channels — the most common instinct — end up rebuilding.

**Step 1: Define the ideal customer profile and segment (week 1–2).** Choose the beachhead segment: the group with the most acute version of the problem, reachable through channels you can afford, with buying authority and budget. Write the ICP as falsifiable criteria — firmographics, situation, trigger events — not adjectives. The full method is in [How to Define Your ICP and Positioning in a GTM Strategy](/insights/gtm-positioning-icp).

**Step 2: Write the positioning (week 2–3).** For the chosen segment: what category does the buyer place you in, what is the alternative, what is the differentiated value, and what proof exists? Positioning is a decision, not copywriting — the copy comes later. Test it in five to ten real customer conversations before locking it.

**Step 3: Set pricing and packaging (week 3–4).** Pricing is a GTM decision because it determines the viable motion. Price anchors to the value delivered and the alternative's cost, not to your costs. Packaging determines the entry point: what does the smallest credible purchase look like, and what does expansion look like? Document the assumptions, because pricing set in week 4 will be revised by month 6 — the goal is a defensible starting point, not a final answer.

**Step 4: Choose the revenue motion (week 4).** Product-led, inbound plus inside sales, outbound-led, partner-led, or founder-led to start. The choice is mostly determined by arithmetic: average contract value against cost per acquisition against sales cycle length. Deals under roughly €5,000 annual value rarely support human-touch sales; deals over €50,000 rarely close without it. Match the motion to the economics, not to the playbook of a company you admire.

**Step 5: Select and sequence channels (week 5).** Pick one primary channel and one secondary — not five. The selection criteria: where does the ICP already pay attention, what can you execute credibly with current resources, and what can you measure? Sequencing beats parallelism at launch because concentrated spend produces interpretable results. Channel-by-channel trade-offs are covered in [Go-to-Market Channels and Lead Generation](/insights/gtm-channels-lead-generation).

**Step 6: Define metrics, targets, and review cadence (week 5–6).** For each stage of the funnel, set a target and a minimum threshold that triggers review. Agree who reviews the numbers, at what cadence, and with authority to change what. A strategy without a review mechanism is a snapshot, not a system.

**Step 7: Build the launch plan and enablement (week 6–8).** Only now does the familiar launch checklist appear: messaging assets, sales enablement material, campaign builds, PR, partner briefings. Everything in this step should trace back to a decision made in steps 1–6. If an asset cannot name which segment and which positioning claim it serves, it is decoration.

## What Belongs in the GTM Strategy Document

The document itself should be short enough that people actually read it — ten to fifteen pages or the equivalent in a wiki, not fifty. The structure that works:

1. **Executive summary** — the strategy in ten sentences: segment, problem, positioning, motion, primary channel, price point, revenue target, and the biggest risk.
2. **Market and problem definition** — the evidence base: market size honestly estimated, the problem, the status quo alternative, and trigger events that create buying moments.
3. **Ideal customer profile** — the falsifiable criteria, the buying committee map, and explicit exclusions: who you will not sell to yet, and why.
4. **Positioning and messaging** — category, alternative, differentiation, proof points, and the messaging hierarchy per audience member (user, buyer, committee).
5. **Pricing and packaging** — the model, the tiers, the discounting policy, and the assumptions behind them.
6. **Revenue motion and funnel design** — the primary motion, the handoffs between marketing, sales, and success, and qualification criteria in writing.
7. **Channel plan** — primary and secondary channels, budget per channel, and the hypothesis each channel is testing.
8. **Metrics and targets** — funnel targets, CAC and payback expectations, review cadence, and pre-registered pivot triggers.
9. **Launch timeline and owners** — who does what, by when, with a single accountable owner per workstream.
10. **Risks and open questions** — everything you knowingly do not know. This section earns more credibility with leadership than any other.

Two structural rules. First, every section states its assumptions explicitly, because the document's second job — after alignment — is to make disagreements with reality diagnosable. Second, the document names one owner. GTM strategies owned by committees are owned by nobody.

## How Long Does It Take?

The honest answer: **four to eight weeks** for a company with existing customer evidence, and **eight to sixteen weeks** for a genuinely new product in a new market where the evidence must be gathered first. The variance is almost entirely in steps 1–2 — segmentation and positioning — because those depend on customer conversations that cannot be compressed below the speed at which buyers agree to talk.

Three timeline traps are worth naming. **The two-week GTM** is usually a channel plan wearing a strategy costume — it skips the customer evidence and inherits whatever assumptions were lying around. **The six-month GTM** is usually an alignment failure disguised as diligence: the document loops through stakeholder reviews because no one has authority to decide. If your GTM strategy is in its fourth review cycle, you have a governance problem, not a strategy problem. **The launched-but-never-revisited GTM** treats the plan as finished on launch day, when launch is actually the moment the plan starts generating evidence. Budget as much energy for the first ninety days of post-launch iteration as for the pre-launch build.

Staffing shapes the timeline as much as scope does. The build needs one accountable owner with genuine cross-functional access — typically a marketing or product-marketing lead in an established company, a founder in an early one — plus committed hours, not just opinions, from sales and product. Customer conversations are the schedule's critical path, so book them in week one for the entire six weeks; teams that schedule interviews reactively lose two to three weeks to calendar friction alone. External help — a consultant or fractional operator — compresses the timeline mainly at steps 2 and 3, where pattern recognition across companies substitutes for first-principles derivation, but it cannot substitute for the team's own customer evidence.

A workable default for a resource-constrained team: six weeks, time-boxed, with steps 1–2 given half the calendar, and a pre-scheduled thirty-day post-launch review where the strategy is amended against real data. Imperfect and shipped beats comprehensive and stale — provided the review actually happens.

If you are building a GTM strategy for the German-speaking markets specifically, the structural differences — longer evaluation cycles, committee buying, compliance-first positioning — change several of these steps materially; the [DACH product launch playbook](/insights/product-launch-dach-playbook) covers them in depth.
