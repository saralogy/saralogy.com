Most companies cannot answer the question "is our go-to-market strategy working?" with data. They can report activity — campaigns shipped, leads generated, calls made — and they can report outcomes — revenue against target. What they cannot do is connect the two, which means when revenue misses, the diagnosis is guesswork and the remedy is whatever the most senior person in the room believes.

This article covers the measurement layer of GTM: which metrics matter at each funnel stage, how to measure customer acquisition cost without fooling yourself, why time-to-revenue deserves more attention than it gets, and how to assemble these into a working verdict on the strategy itself.

## The Metrics That Matter, Stage by Stage

The instinct to track everything produces dashboards nobody reads. A GTM measurement system needs roughly a dozen numbers, chosen so that each funnel stage has one volume metric, one quality metric, and one velocity metric.

**Awareness and demand.** Volume: qualified traffic and reach within the ICP — not total traffic; visits from outside your target segment are noise wearing a growth costume. Quality: engagement depth from ICP accounts (return visits, content consumption, branded search growth). Velocity: cost per qualified visit trending over time. For B2B specifically, measuring content against pipeline rather than pageviews changes which content gets made — the framework in [Content KPIs for B2B Marketing in Germany](/insights/content-kpis-b2b-germany) applies directly.

**Lead and opportunity creation.** Volume: qualified leads or product signups matching ICP criteria. Quality: lead-to-opportunity conversion rate — the single best test of whether marketing and sales agree on what "qualified" means. If sales converts fewer than 20–30% of "qualified" leads into real opportunities, the qualification definition is broken, not the sales team. Velocity: time from first touch to opportunity.

**Sales conversion.** Volume: opportunities created per month. Quality: win rate against your primary named competitor and win rate against "no decision" — tracked separately, because they diagnose different diseases. Losing to a competitor is a positioning or product problem; losing to no-decision is an urgency and ICP problem. Velocity: sales cycle length by segment.

**Retention and expansion.** GTM measurement does not stop at closed-won. Volume: net revenue retention. Quality: onboarding completion and time-to-first-value. Velocity: months to expansion. Early churn — customers leaving inside the first ninety days — is not a customer success failure; it is a GTM failure. It means the strategy acquired buyers the product was not built for, and it is the strongest signal that ICP or positioning needs revision.

Two disciplines make this stack useful. Segment every metric by ICP cohort — blended numbers hide the fact that one segment is thriving and another is draining budget. And record targets before launch, because a number without an expectation attached is trivia.

## Measuring Customer Acquisition Cost Properly

CAC is the most quoted and most miscalculated number in go-to-market. The definition is simple — total sales and marketing cost divided by new customers acquired in a period — but the miscalculations are systematic and they all flatter the business.

**Include salaries and overhead, not just media spend.** A CAC that counts ad spend but excludes the salaries of the people running the ads, the SDR team, and the sales engineers is a fiction. Fully loaded CAC is typically two to four times media-only CAC. If the fully loaded number is unflattering, that is precisely why it must be the number you use.

**Respect the time lag.** Customers closed this quarter were generated by spend from previous quarters. In a business with a four-month sales cycle, dividing this quarter's spend by this quarter's new customers misattributes systematically — it punishes you when scaling up and flatters you when cutting. Match cohorts: spend from the period that actually generated these customers.

**Calculate CAC by channel and by segment, not just blended.** Blended CAC is a board metric; operational decisions need the split. It is entirely normal for paid CAC to be three times organic CAC — the question is what the marginal euro achieves, and only channel-level CAC answers it. The attribution mechanics behind this are their own discipline; [B2B Marketing Attribution in Germany](/insights/marketing-attribution-b2b-germany) covers what is realistically measurable under GDPR conditions.

**Pair CAC with payback period, not just LTV:CAC.** LTV:CAC ratios depend on lifetime-value assumptions that early-stage companies cannot yet support with data. CAC payback — months of gross margin needed to recover acquisition cost — uses only observed numbers. Under twelve months is generally healthy for SMB-focused businesses; enterprise motions tolerate eighteen to twenty-four. Payback trending upward while win rates hold is an early warning that a channel is saturating.

## Time-to-Revenue: The Number Most Teams Never Calculate

Time-to-revenue is the elapsed time from GTM investment to recognised revenue — and at launch, from project kickoff to first repeatable sales. It is the metric that best captures whether the GTM strategy as a whole is efficient, and almost nobody tracks it deliberately.

Why it matters: two GTM strategies can have identical CAC and win rates, and one can still be dramatically better because it reaches revenue in four months instead of eleven. Faster time-to-revenue compounds — earlier revenue funds earlier iteration, produces earlier customer evidence, and de-risks the next planning cycle. For a company with finite runway, time-to-revenue is closer to a survival metric than a performance metric.

What lengthens it, in rough order of impact: choosing an enterprise motion before you have enterprise proof points; launching to a segment with no urgency trigger, so deals stall at "interesting, not now"; positioning that requires educating the market on the problem before selling the solution; and onboarding friction that delays the value moment after signature — which delays references, which delays the next cohort.

The GTM strategy's job is to shorten this deliberately: a beachhead segment with an active trigger event, an entry-level package that lowers the first-purchase threshold, and a channel where buyers already congregate all trade some theoretical market size for speed. That trade is almost always correct at launch. Measure it simply: date-stamp the strategy's approval, first marketing activity, first opportunity, first closed deal, and first repeat pattern (three deals with the same shape). Review the intervals quarterly.

## Assembling the Verdict: Is the Strategy Working?

Individual metrics diagnose components; the strategy verdict needs them assembled. A GTM strategy is working when four conditions hold simultaneously:

1. **The ICP is confirmed by behaviour.** The segment you targeted is the segment actually converting — and closed customers match the profile you wrote down. If your best customers keep arriving from a segment you did not target, the strategy is wrong in an informative way.
2. **The funnel converts within planned tolerances at every stage.** Not perfectly — within tolerances you set in advance. One broken stage is a tactics problem. Three broken stages is a strategy problem.
3. **Unit economics close.** Fully loaded CAC pays back inside the target window, and early retention shows the revenue is durable rather than borrowed.
4. **The motion is becoming more repeatable, not less.** Win rates, cycle lengths, and CAC should improve or hold as volume grows. Metrics that degrade with scale mean the early results were founder effect or early-adopter effect, not strategy.

Cadence matters as much as the metrics: weekly for funnel operations, monthly for channel economics, quarterly for the strategy-level verdict. Reviewing strategy weekly produces thrash; reviewing it annually produces archaeology. The quarterly review works best as a standing ritual with a fixed agenda — the four conditions above, each answered with evidence, each answer logged — so that the verdict is a comparison across quarters rather than a fresh debate each time.

When the verdict is negative, resist the reflex to change everything. The stack above is designed to localise the failure: behaviour-confirmed ICP but broken conversion points to positioning or enablement; good conversion but unaffordable CAC points to channel or pricing; everything green except retention points to a product-market gap that marketing cannot fix. The signals that justify a genuine pivot — as opposed to iteration — are covered in [Why Go-to-Market Strategies Fail](/insights/why-go-to-market-strategies-fail).

Finally, a note on sample sizes, because it changes how these verdicts should be read in B2B. At ten or twenty deals a quarter, no single month's conversion rate means much; the discipline is to judge trends across rolling quarters and to weight qualitative evidence — win/loss interviews, verbatim objections, competitive mentions — alongside the numbers. A team that closes eight deals in a quarter learns more from eight structured win interviews than from any dashboard. The quantitative stack tells you where to look; the conversations tell you what you are looking at. Treating small-sample metrics as statistically decisive produces confident, wrong pivots — the most expensive failure mode in early GTM measurement.

None of this requires enterprise tooling. A disciplined CRM, clean stage definitions, and a spreadsheet outperform a neglected six-figure stack; if the tooling itself is the bottleneck, start with [a lean marketing analytics stack](/insights/marketing-analytics-lean-stack) and add complexity only when a specific decision demands it. The companies that answer "is our GTM working?" confidently are rarely the ones with the most metrics — they are the ones whose dozen numbers were chosen before launch, measured honestly, and reviewed on a cadence nobody skips.
