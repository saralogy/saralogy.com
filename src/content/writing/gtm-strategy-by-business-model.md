"What's the best go-to-market strategy?" is an underspecified question. GTM strategy is downstream of business model: the price point, the buyer, the purchase process, and the value-delivery mechanics determine which motions are even viable before any creativity enters the picture. A strategy that is excellent for a €30-a-month SaaS product is malpractice for a €300,000 enterprise platform, and vice versa.

This article covers the three model distinctions that come up most: what works for SaaS companies specifically, the structural differences between B2B and B2C go-to-market, and what enterprise sales demands that no other motion does.

## The Best GTM Strategy for SaaS Companies

There is no single best SaaS GTM strategy — but there is a reliable way to determine yours, because SaaS motions sort cleanly by the interaction of price point and product complexity.

**Product-led growth (PLG)** — self-serve signup, free tier or trial, upgrade paths inside the product — fits when the product delivers a felt "aha" within minutes to hours, a single user can adopt it without permission, and entry pricing sits roughly below €100 per month. The GTM strategy's centre of gravity shifts into the product itself: onboarding friction, time-to-value, and in-product expansion prompts are the funnel. Marketing's job becomes driving qualified signups cheaply; sales, if present at all, works the accounts where usage signals expansion. The failure mode is choosing PLG for aspirational reasons — because it looks capital-efficient — when the product needs configuration, data migration, or cross-team adoption before value appears. PLG with a slow time-to-value is a machine for manufacturing abandoned trials.

**Inbound plus inside sales** fits the broad middle: contract values from roughly €5,000 to €50,000 annually, a considered purchase with two to five stakeholders, value that benefits from a demonstration. Content and demand generation create qualified pipeline; inside sales converts it over a two-week to three-month cycle. This is the default motion for European B2B SaaS, and its economics live or die on the qualification handshake between marketing and sales described in [How to Align Product, Sales and Marketing on GTM](/insights/gtm-alignment-sales-marketing-product).

**Outbound-led and enterprise motions** take over above roughly €50,000 in annual contract value, where the buying committee grows and deals justify human effort per account — covered in the enterprise section below.

Two SaaS-specific rules cut across all three motions. First, the motion must match the arithmetic: fully loaded cost per acquisition against contract value against payback tolerance. A €3,000 ACV cannot fund an account executive's time; a €150,000 ACV will not close through a signup form. Second, hybrid is the norm at scale but poison at launch — successful SaaS companies usually end up layering PLG, inbound, and outbound, but they start with one, prove it repeatable, then add. Starting with three motions splits focus and produces three inconclusive experiments.

## B2B vs B2C: The Structural Differences

B2B and B2C go-to-market differ not by degree but by structure — five mechanics change, and each changes the strategy's shape.

**Who decides.** B2C has one decision-maker (occasionally a household); B2B has a committee where the user, the payer, and the evaluators are different people with different objections. Consequence: B2C messaging optimises one persuasion moment; B2B GTM must arm an internal champion to re-sell the product in meetings you will never attend. The quality of your champion-enablement material — the business case, the one-pager, the security documentation — is a B2B GTM asset with no B2C equivalent.

**How the decision is made.** B2C purchases are fast, emotional, and individually low-stakes; B2B purchases are slow, justified, and career-relevant — the buyer is partly managing personal risk ("what if this fails and it was my recommendation?"). Consequence: B2C GTM buys attention and reduces friction; B2B GTM manufactures trust and reduces perceived risk — references, proof, pilots, compliance posture. Risk-reduction assets convert in B2B the way discounts convert in B2C.

**Unit economics.** B2C works on small margins across enormous volumes, so GTM lives and dies on efficient mass channels and conversion-rate optimisation; a euro of CAC waste multiplied by a million customers is catastrophic. B2B works on large values across small volumes, so GTM tolerates high per-account costs and dies instead by leaky qualification — one quarter of sales time spent on wrong-fit accounts is the B2B equivalent of a broken checkout page.

**Channels.** B2C reaches consumers where they already are: paid social, retail, marketplaces, television. B2B reaches buyers through professional surfaces: search at the moment of problem-awareness, LinkedIn, trade press, events, and — most underrated — the buyer's peer network. In German-speaking B2B markets this divergence is even sharper; [LinkedIn's role in German B2B](/insights/linkedin-b2b-germany-2026) has no consumer analogue.

**Measurement.** B2C attribution is near-real-time and statistically dense — thousands of conversions make A/B tests conclusive in days. B2B operates on dozens of deals per quarter with multi-month lags; statistical significance is a luxury, and measurement leans on cohort logic and deal-level evidence instead, as covered in [Go-to-Market Metrics](/insights/go-to-market-metrics).

The practical warning: hybrid models — B2B2C, marketplaces, prosumer products — need two GTM strategies with explicit sequencing, not an average of the two. Averaged strategies inherit the costs of both structures and the advantages of neither.

## GTM for Enterprise Sales

Enterprise GTM — contract values from six figures up, buying committees of five to fifteen people, cycles of six to eighteen months — is not the mid-market motion with bigger numbers. Several mechanics exist only here.

**Account-based everything.** The addressable market is a countable list — often two hundred to two thousand named accounts — which inverts the funnel logic: instead of generating demand broadly and qualifying down, you select accounts first and orchestrate marketing, sales, and executive attention against each. Account selection quality is therefore the highest-leverage decision in enterprise GTM; a tiered list (ten pursued deeply, fifty warmed, the rest monitored for trigger events) outperforms uniform coverage every time.

**The procurement gauntlet as a designed-for stage.** Security review, legal review, data protection assessment, procurement negotiation, and in DACH frequently works-council consultation — these are not friction after the sale; they are stages of the sale, each with a stakeholder who can veto and a document they expect to exist. Enterprise GTM readiness means those artefacts — security whitepaper, DPA, compliance certifications, reference architecture — are built before the pipeline needs them. A deal that reaches security review without a security story loses six months or dies.

**The champion is the sales channel.** Most of the selling in an enterprise deal happens internally, by your champion, in your absence. The GTM strategy must treat champion enablement as a first-class workstream: the business case they will present, the objection responses they will need, the internal-politics map of who must be won and who must merely not object.

**Land-and-expand as pricing architecture.** Few enterprises buy the full vision upfront. The motion that works prices a credible landing point — a division, a use case, a pilot with contractual success criteria — and designs the expansion path into the account plan from day one. Enterprise GTM economics usually only close on the expanded account, which means net revenue retention is the motion's true success metric, not initial deal size.

**Patience, funded.** An enterprise motion needs twelve to twenty-four months of runway before the pipeline proves itself, plus senior sellers who cost six figures each. The most common enterprise GTM failure is not losing deals — it is companies attempting the motion with nine months of budget, which is a decision to fail slowly. If the funding does not match the cycle, the honest strategy is to enter through the mid-market motion and earn the enterprise proof points first.

## Choosing When Your Model Sits Between Categories

Plenty of real companies do not fit cleanly into one of these boxes: the €4,000 ACV product that is slightly too expensive for pure self-serve and slightly too cheap for human sales; the prosumer tool bought by individuals and expensed by companies; the mid-market product with occasional six-figure enterprise interest. For these, three tie-breaking principles apply.

**Let the buyer's behaviour, not the price point, cast the deciding vote.** If trials convert without human contact, the motion is product-led regardless of what the pricing spreadsheet suggests; if every meaningful deal involves a conversation, staff for the conversation. Run both paths for a quarter, measure conversion and fully loaded cost per path, and commit to the winner as primary.

**Solve the awkward-middle ACV with packaging, not with a hybrid sales team.** A €4,000 product struggling between motions usually needs its packaging pulled apart: a genuine self-serve tier at a price that needs no salesperson, and a team tier priced high enough (€10,000-plus) to fund the sales effort it requires. Splitting the offer is almost always cheaper than building a sales motion that loses money on half its deals.

**Treat out-of-model demand as a signal to bank, not a motion to build.** The mid-market company that receives occasional enterprise interest should take the deals opportunistically — founder-led, honestly scoped — while resisting the gravitational pull to rebuild the GTM around them until the enterprise pattern repeats and the funding for a real enterprise motion exists. One large logo is an anecdote; five with the same shape is a strategy input.

Across all three models the meta-rule is the same: the business model chooses the motion; the team's job is to accept the choice and execute it deeply, rather than borrowing tactics from companies whose economics they do not share.
